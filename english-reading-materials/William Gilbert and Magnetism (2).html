William Gilbert and Magnetism

A The 16th and 17th centuries saw two great pioneers of modern science: Galileo and Gilbert. The impact of their findings is eminent. Gilbert was the first modern scientist, also the accredited father of the science of electricity and magnetism, an Englishman of learning and a physician to Queen Elizabeth. He died in 1603 and was buried in his hometown of Colchester.

B William Gilbert was born in Colchester but would later attend St John's College, Cambridge where he received his education, eventually graduating with an MD and shortly after heading the Royal College of Physicians. He came to the court of Queen Elizabeth in 1601 and remained there for the last three years of her life. It was not unusual for scientists to also be physicians in Gilbert's time. In fact, it was more the rule than the exception. The reason for this was that physicians occupied a relatively high position in society and so were encouraged to explore all areas of knowledge. The practice of medicine in Gilbert's time was of a very basic nature and doctors did not as a rule conduct experimental research. Gilbert was different.

C Gilbert was interested in chemistry and followed the Copernican system of the universe. As a natural philosopher, he moved away from the theories of aristotelianism to become one of the first genuine scientists in the modern sense of the word. He was a pioneer in developing the scientific method of research and observed before concluding. He was meticulous in his record-keeping and made careful notes on all the experiments he conducted.

D Gilbert was also a major figure in establishing the foundations of the study of electricity and magnetism as a science. He was the first to use the terms electric attraction, electrical force, and magnetic pole. He also coined the New Latin word 'electricus' to describe the property of attracting small objects after being rubbed. The English word 'electricity' derives from it. In 1600, he published his findings in a treatise entitled 'De Magnete' (On the Magnet). The work was well received in England where the people were at that time interested in practical science, but less so in Europe where traditional Aristotelian philosophy was still dominant. The work was also perhaps the first comprehensive book of physical science to be written in England.

E In De Magnete, Gilbert described many of his experiments with his model Earth called the terrella. Gilbert discovered that if you place a piece of iron on a lodestone, the iron becomes magnetised and behaves like a magnet, attracting other pieces of iron. He also found that if you heat a magnet to a very high temperature, it loses its magnetism. Gilbert correctly deduced that the center of the Earth was iron and that a compass needle is attracted to it. Gilbert was the first to understand that the Earth itself is a big magnet and he explained that this is why a compass needle points north.

F From his experiments, Gilbert also concluded that the tail of the Orion constellation was further away from the Earth than its head. He arrived at this conclusion by assuming that the Earth was at the center of the universe. He theorized that fixed stars were at remote but varying distances although he didn't publish this information until 1610. This was in contrast to the general belief at the time that all fixed stars were at a uniform distance from the Earth. It was only after his death that his papers were examined and the discovery made that he had arrived at this conclusion a few years before his book was published.

G Gilbert's findings laid the groundwork for Galileo's later discoveries that the Earth and other planets revolve around the sun. In fact, Galileo praised Gilbert in several of his own works. Gilbert was also influential in the fields of cartography and exploration. He argued that the North-West Passage across the top of North America existed and could be navigated. This passage was eventually discovered in 1851. Gilbert's work on magnetism was so influential that when the first galvanometer, an instrument for detecting and measuring electric current, was invented in 1824, the unit of measurement was named the 'gilbert' in his honor. However, this unit has since been superseded by the ampere.
